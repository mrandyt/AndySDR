# AndySDR

A desktop SDR scanner for Windows 10/11 with a classic hardware-scanner front panel, a live
spectrum/waterfall, 1000 memory channels, a band search that finds every live transmission,
RadioReference UK CSV import, digital/analogue decoding, weather pictures, and a built-in
network server so any other PC, laptop, tablet or phone in the house can use the radio.

**Latest: 1.31.2** – **the Northwood WEFAX memory channel is on the carrier, not the published figure.** It was stored at 8040.000 kHz, 1.9 kHz above where the chart is actually sent, so the fax tones fell outside the decoder and no chart could be decoded. It now sits at **8038.100 kHz USB**. A saved copy of that channel is corrected automatically the first time 1.31.2 starts; any channel you renamed or retuned yourself is left alone.

**Before that: 1.31.1** – **every pass now checks whether the receiver is really on frequency, and says which kind of error it is.** A satellite pass is the one time a receiver is handed a signal whose true frequency is known in advance: the orbit says where the carrier will be, second by second. Any difference is error, and measuring it right through a pass says *which* error – which one reading never can. **A constant offset is the receiver's own crystal**, the same proportion at every frequency, and a ppm correction cures it; AndySDR works out the figure and tells you what to set. **An offset that swings with the satellite's speed** is the orbit prediction, usually orbit data that wants refreshing, and a ppm correction would make every other band worse. **An offset that creeps one way through the pass** is the crystal warming up. At 137.9 MHz one part per million is 138 Hz, and the same error is 11 kHz at 1090 MHz, which is why an oscillator a few ppm out shows itself on ADS-B long before anywhere else. The measurement does not hunt for a peak – an LRPT carrier is 120 kHz wide and flat on top, so its peak wanders with the noise; it slides a block the width of the signal across the spectrum, takes the fullest position and then finds the middle of the power inside it, which reads finer than one bin. Both receivers are checked independently, and nothing is concluded from a weak pass: scattered readings are reported as scattered rather than turned into a correction.

**Before that: 1.31.0** – **three faults found by using it rather than reading it.** *The RECEIVER 2 panel still would not appear* – because the fix before last mended the wide layout and left the narrow one alone, so on a window under 1100px the panel was auto-placed into a row of its own several thousand pixels below the page. A layout test now reads the stylesheet and checks that **every** `.wrap` layout has a square for every panel, because this cannot be seen in one browser at one window size, which is exactly how it survived two attempts. *Auto ID flip-flopped between AM and FM* about once a second on ACARS and switched the demodulator each time – demodulating AM as FM, which sounds like bad distortion. ACARS is silence most of the time and the classifier was reading the gaps, where noise is as likely to look like FM as AM. Only readings taken while there is a signal now get a vote, a run of four is needed, there is a quiet period after each change, and if it still changes its mind repeatedly on one frequency it stops and says so. *TETRA was announced on anything over 15 kHz wide* – which is most of a 12.5 kHz channel measured generously, so ordinary business radio on 165, 168, 454 and 469 MHz was being called TETRA and a decoder started that could never produce anything. Measured on those real signals the false ones ran 15.1 to 18.6 kHz; real TETRA on 380.7875 MHz measured 20.7. The bar is raised to where a 12.5 kHz system cannot reach, the envelope has a ceiling as well as a floor, and outside the bands TETRA is actually allocated in it is described as what it is – a wideband digital carrier – with no decoder started. On the emergency-services band it now says plainly that the air interface is encrypted and neither speech nor data will decode, which saves an evening.

**Before that: 1.30.2** – **the automatic gain no longer takes range off a decoder that needs the gain left alone.** “Ease RF gain down on strong signals” had no idea a decoder was running. Two things were wrong with that. ADS-B has no gain of its own – it uses whatever the main radio is set to – so every step the automatic gain took came straight off its range, and the narrowband channel level it was reading at 1090 MHz told it nothing useful in the first place. And the step size was written as a fraction of the receiver's gain range: on a dongle that is a gentle 3 dB and a 25 dB limit, but the SDRplay's range is 0–100, so the same fractions gave **6 dB jumps and a 50 dB drop** – not easing anything, just going deaf. It now stays out of the way entirely for anything handed raw IQ (ADS-B, MeshCore, the sensor bands, AERO, the satellites) and for anything where the amplitude *is* the information (ACARS is AM; a WEFAX or SSTV picture is drawn by amplitude, so moving the gain part way through puts a band across it). Steps are capped at 3 dB and the total drop at 20 dB whatever the receiver's range. What is left is what it was written for – FM voice, pagers, packet – where only the frequency carries meaning and backing off a distorting signal genuinely helps.

**Before that: 1.30.1** – **a timed capture that duplicates a tracked satellite now says so, and offers to switch itself off.** The schedule already warned when two captures overlapped. What it could not say is that two of them were the same job. A daily capture on 137.900 MHz from 03:46 for twenty-five minutes held the radio until 04:11, so the Meteor-M N2-4 pass that began at 04:05 was recorded from 04:11 to 04:15 – four minutes of ten. That is not an ordinary clash: the timer cannot follow the satellite, it records the quiet minutes either side whether or not anything is overhead, and because it starts first it takes the pass away from the tracking that would have done it properly. AndySDR now recognises a timed capture aimed at the same frequency and the same job as a satellite it already tracks, says plainly that switching it off loses nothing, and puts the button next to the sentence. It says it when the capture is created, too, which is the cheapest moment to think again.

**Before that: 1.30.0** – **AndySDR can follow a trunked system.** On a trunked network the talking is not on the frequency you are listening to: one channel carries nothing but signalling, and when somebody presses the key it says, in effect, *“this call is on channel 17 for the next half minute”*. Sit on the control channel and you hear data; sit on a voice channel and you catch one call in ten. Following a conversation means moving to each call's channel and back within a fraction of a second. DSD-FME already knows how to read those grants – what it needs is something that will retune when it asks, over the **rigctl** protocol that SDR++ and Hamlib radios speak. AndySDR now is that something. Leave the radio on the control channel, pick one of the new **Trunked** sub-modes (DMR Tier III / Capacity+ / Connect+, P25, P25 Phase 2, NXDN) and each call is followed and dropped automatically. **Where the call lands inside the slice the receiver is already capturing, nothing is retuned at all** – the radio just listens to a different part of what it already has, so there is no delay and no clipped start of a call. That is where a wide receiver earns its keep: an RSP2 at 7.2 MS/s can hold a whole site at once. Most DMR systems need a channel map, because the control channel talks in channel numbers rather than frequencies, so there is a box to paste one into (451200000 and 451.2 both work) along with an optional talkgroup-name list; P25 systems usually need neither, because they broadcast their frequencies. The panel shows how many calls have been followed and where the last one was, which is the quickest way to tell whether a map is right. The tuning server listens to this machine only and runs only while a trunked sub-mode is selected, because anything that can reach it could retune the radio.

**Before that: 1.29.0** – **every pass now measures what signal it actually took to decode.** The report used to give the peak and the typical signal, neither of which answers the question that decides whether a pass is worth recording. Every Meteor frame carries a number that counts up whether or not you hear it, so AndySDR can now say how many of the frames the satellite sent while it was in reach actually arrived – 55% means four frames in ten were missed – and that is exact, resting on nothing. Those same numbers are a clock, so the run of frames can be laid over the signal trace from the same pass, and the new **Frames** column also shows the weakest signal that was still producing them: *“55% · to 2.7 dB”*. That is the real threshold for this receiver and this aerial, measured on the day rather than guessed at from whether pictures came out, and it is the number to watch when an aerial, a filter or a lead changes. It is left blank when too few frames arrived to place them in time, or when they arrived too steadily to tell one moment from another – no figure is better than an invented one. The `.cadu` files of every pass already recorded are read once at startup too, so the coverage figures for the existing archive appear straight away; the threshold only starts from passes recorded from here on, because the signal traces of the older ones were never kept.

**Before that: 1.28.2** – **the gain test now says how much gain is too much, and gives up instead of hanging.** Its sweep already measured the whole curve, but it only looked for the lower bend – the point where you start hearing the aerial rather than the receiver. There is usually a second bend near the top, where the noise floor stops following the gain again: that one is the front end beginning to compress, and it is common behind a mast preamplifier. The test now finds it and reports it – *“do not go above 39 dB on this aerial”* – never recommends a gain above it, and counts headroom up to it rather than to the top of the dial – or up to the overload point, whichever comes first, since a receiver that has told you it is overloading has 0 dB left in hand, not 67. Readings taken above that overload point are ignored when looking for the ceiling: a real RSP2 sweep has the floor dropping 40 dB there and then leaping back, and none of that is a measurement. On the 137 MHz dongle the measured bend is 38.6 dB, which is why 40 dB was a poor setting there. Two other fixes: if the receiver stops sending samples part way through a sweep – another copy of the program has it open, or a lead is knocked – the test now gives up after eight seconds, puts the gain back and says so on screen, instead of sitting at “step 1 of 22” with the radio deaf; and the **RECEIVER 2 panel now actually appears**, having been placed off the bottom of the page by the screen layout rather than between the spectrum and the tabs where it belongs.

**Before that: 1.28.0** – **both receivers are now on screen at once, each named**. The second receiver used to live inside the satellite page, so the main screen looked like a one-radio program – and the figure beside the receiver's name in the header, “1 connected”, read as though only one of the two was working when it was really counting screens. Now: the header names both radios (“SDRplay RSP2 (180800E611) + Nooelec NESDR SMArt v5”), the spectrum is labelled **RECEIVER 1** with its hardware name, and a permanent **RECEIVER 2** panel sits underneath with its own name, a coloured state dot (grey off, green awake, blue live view, red recording), the pass it is waiting for, live signal, gain and elevation, and its own trace and waterfall. The screen count only appears when somebody else really is connected, and then says so in words – “also open on 1 other screen”.

**Before that: 1.27.3** – **the gain test no longer drops the gain to nothing when there is a preamplifier in front**. Its rule was “use the lowest gain at which the noise floor follows the gain”, which is right when that bend sits somewhere in the receiver's range. On the 137 MHz dongle, which has a powered LNA ahead of it, the floor follows the gain from the very first step: the aerial is already louder than the dongle everywhere, so the bend is below the range entirely. The test read that correctly and then drew the wrong conclusion, setting the gain to 0 dB. It now recognises that case, keeps the gain where it was, and says so – that nothing in the sweep makes one setting better than another for sensitivity, and that overload at the top end is what is worth watching at that aerial instead. The readings are also written to the log now, so a surprising answer can be checked rather than guessed at.

**Before that: 1.27.2** – **the gain test now moves the squelch with it, and the second receiver has one of its own**. Squelch is a level in dBFS, so it only means anything against a particular gain: the first real run of the gain test on a Marine VHF aerial took the gain from 60 dB down to 28.6 dB, which dropped every signal 31 dB and left a squelch nothing could open – the scanner went deaf. The test now shifts the squelch by exactly the amount it shifts the gain, so the same signals go on opening it, and says so in the result. Putting the gain back, or picking a saved one from the list, carries the squelch the same way. A squelch left wide open is left wide open. **Receiver 2 now has its own Find the best gain**, on its own screen: it opens the dongle on 137.900 MHz, sweeps its 29 gain steps and measures the bend, which settles what the right gain for the Meteor aerial actually is instead of judging it off the waterfall. It will not run during a pass.

**Before that: 1.27.1** – **two small things that have each cost an evening**. The *“use both receivers” tick no longer looks as though it changes itself*: there are two of them, the one on the Receiver 2 screen and a per-satellite one in each Meteor's Options on the Scheduler, and the per-satellite one wins whenever it has been touched. When the two disagree the Receiver 2 screen now says which satellites have their own answer, instead of quietly showing a setting nobody is using. And the *Pass report now says how many pictures are worth keeping*, not just the median fill: a pass that shows “76%” can be one good picture and one of static, which “1/2 good” beside it makes plain. Up to 24 pictures a pass are measured now rather than 12.

**Before that: 1.27.0** – **AndySDR can now find the best RF gain for itself, band by band**. There is no single right gain: it depends on the aerial, the band and what else is on the air, which is why a number chosen once and left alone is usually wrong. Settings › Receivers has a **Find the best gain** button. It turns the gain up step by step and watches the noise floor: while the receiver's own noise is the louder of the two the floor barely moves, and once the aerial's noise is the louder it follows the gain one dB for one dB. The bend between them is the answer, and the gain chosen is just above it – the point where you are hearing the aerial and not the receiver, with under half a decibel lost to the receiver. More gain than that improves nothing and only loses headroom. The sweep takes about 15 seconds, uses the receiver's own gain steps where it has them, and refuses to run during a scheduled capture or while scanning. If the receiver reports being overloaded part way up, it stops below that and says so; if the noise floor barely moves at all it says the aerial may be very quiet or not connected rather than quietly picking a number. Every answer is kept against the band and the receiver it was measured on, with a list to put any of them back in one press. 'Ease RF gain down on strong signals' still works on top: this sets the ceiling, that one backs off from it.

**Before that: 1.26.9** – **aircraft messages are now read, not just shown**. An ACARS body is one long run-on line of an airline's own making, and a lot of it is machine data that will never be readable – but a good share of it carries a position, where the flight is going and the navigation points it is passing, and all of that was being thrown away. AndySDR now reads the body and labels what it finds: latitude and longitude in the five layouts that are actually heard, the airports the flight is between, the navigation points with the times against them, and what the message is for (an engine-data download, a datalink logon, an oceanic clearance, a position contract). Each message row shows what was read as small chips under the text, the detail window lays the body out line by line, and the search box looks in the decoded parts too. Measured against a real day's reception (729 messages on 27 Nov, 477 of them with any text): 416 now carry something labelled – 20 positions, 68 routes, 62 sets of navigation points and 380 named applications. Positions are checked against the receiver's own location, so a misread cannot put an aircraft on the other side of the world. Also: the figure at the top of the screen now says “1 screen” rather than “1 connected”, which read as though only one of the two receivers was working.

**Before that: 1.26.8** – **three accuracy fixes found in a review of the decoding and measuring code**. *Signal (dB) was flattering every weak pass*: the channel holds the signal **and** the noise, and the noise was never taken off before the two were compared, so a channel with nothing in it still read about +2 dB and a true 0 dB read +3.4 dB. The error was worst exactly where it matters. The noise is now subtracted first: an empty channel reads about −11 dB, and the figures are within about 0.5 dB of the truth from −3 dB upwards. Figures already in the pass report keep their old values, so passes from before and after are not directly comparable. The same routine used to give no reading at all when the channel was wide compared with the view (a 120 kHz LRPT channel in a 240 kHz view left no room for its guard bands); it now narrows the guard instead of giving up. *A steady tone on FM was read as a pager*: a sine wave's discriminator is flatter-topped than speech, which is the test used to spot 2FSK, so a test tone, a pip, a paging alert or mains hum could be announced as “Pager – POCSAG 2400” and switch the mode. Auto ID now measures how much of the discriminator's power sits in one or two narrow lines (a tone 0.99, real data 0.01–0.17, speech 0.02) and will not call a pure tone a digital system. A second fault in the same place let the pager test run even when the “is this really digital?” check had already failed. The Auto ID benchmark gains 36 new tone readings and still identifies all 60 real-signal cells. *Update checks stopped making a doomed connection first*: on a PC whose own certificate store cannot verify GitHub, every check and every download failed once and then retried with the bundled list; the bundled list is now used for the rest of the session after the first failure.

**Before that: 1.26.7** – **AndySDR can start itself when you sign in to Windows**: a scheduled satellite pass is only recorded while the program is running, so a restart in the night (a Windows update, a power cut) quietly costs a morning's pictures – which is what happened to the 19:36 pass on 27 Nov. Settings now has a tick, “Start AndySDR when I sign in to Windows”, which adds AndySDR to the per-user sign-in start list. No administrator rights, nothing installed, and unticking it takes the entry away again. If an update or a move leaves the entry pointing at a path that is no longer there, start-up quietly puts the right one back. The tick only appears in the installed AndySDR (run from source there is no single command for Windows to start), and a PC left at the lock screen with nobody signed in still will not start it.

**Before that: 1.26.6** – **the picture “Fill” figure no longer counts static as picture**: Fill was the share of scan lines that were not pure black, so a receiver that lost the signal and filled its lines with random speckle still scored high – a pass showing a mostly-static picture was reported as 80.6%. Lines are now also checked for being smooth along the row (a real scan line looks like its neighbouring pixels, static does not), and static lines count as missed. The same real picture now reads 30.2%, and a clean one 76.3%.

**Before that: 1.26.5** – **the “use both receivers” merge now says what happened, and leaves no empty folders**: when a pass was recorded on both receivers, the merge only checked that each one had produced a frames file – not that there was anything in it. A receiver that hears nothing still writes an empty file, so the check passed, a `_merged` folder was created, and the merge then found it had only one usable receiver, gave up and left the folder empty with nothing in it to say why. Every merged folder made so far was one of these. The frames are now counted first: no folder is created unless two receivers each heard at least one frame, and the pass log names the receiver that heard nothing instead of reporting a failure. The wait for the slower receiver also starts when the first one finishes rather than when the pass began, so a long pass followed by a slow SatDump run can no longer use up the whole wait before either receiver has reported and throw away a pass that could have been merged. Checked against a real pass (254 frames on one receiver, none on the other) and by splitting those same 254 real frames across two receivers fading at different times – 170 and 134 frames, merged back to the full 254, 49% more than the better receiver alone. Changes to the second receiver's settings are now written to the log with the old value, the new value and who asked, so a setting that turns up holding a value nobody remembers choosing can be traced.

**1.26.4** – **MUTE / AUDIO now work reliably**: there were two separate mute switches – the big AUDIO key and the MUTE quick button – that did not know about each other. Muting with the quick button and then pressing the AUDIO key closed the sound and reopened it, but the mute was still set, so the key read MUTED and stayed silent however often it was pressed. They are now one switch: the first press starts the audio, after that it mutes and un-mutes at once (the audio is kept running, not closed and rebuilt), the radio stops sending sound to a muted screen, and the key and the quick button always show the same state. Checked in a real browser by measuring the sound at the output after each press, in every mix of the key and the quick button (the old version fails that test).

**1.26.3** – **no more jumping panels**: the green Auto-ID chip ("◉ DMR voice → decoding") used to appear and disappear as the signal came and went, and each time the rows around it grew and shrank - the Decoders log and the bar above the spectrum jumped up and down. The chip now keeps its place (hidden or shown, the row is the same height), a signal that drops out for a moment keeps its label for 5 seconds instead of flickering (it still clears at once when you retune), long names are cut with "…" rather than wrapping onto a second line, and the decoder log adds each new line instead of rebuilding the whole log every time. Checked in the real page: bar and log heights identical with no signal, a named signal and a very long name.

**1.26.2** – **the frequency no longer wanders**: the radio's keyboard shortcuts (▲▼ step the frequency, 0-9 . Enter, S M P L N) used to work anywhere on the page, so scrolling a long tab with the arrow keys moved the tuning by one step (12.5 kHz: 168.0000 ↔ 167.9875 MHz), and a stray S, M or P could start a scan or store a channel. They now work only while the mouse pointer is over the radio or the spectrum; anywhere else the keys do their normal job (▲▼ scroll, Space/Enter press the focused button), and Ctrl/Alt/⌘ combinations are never taken. Checked with the real page: arrow keys away from the radio left the frequency alone and scrolled the page, over the radio they still step. ## Getting the .exe

**Easiest – let GitHub build it for you (no installs on your PC):**
1. Create a free GitHub account, make a new repository, and upload this whole folder (including the hidden `.github` folder).
2. Open the repository's **Actions** tab – the "Build AndySDR for Windows" job runs automatically (about 10 minutes).
3. Download the **AndySDR-windows** artifact: it contains `AndySDR-windows.zip` (unzip and run `AndySDR.exe`) and `AndySDR-Setup.exe` (installer with Start-menu shortcut and firewall rule).

**Or build on your own PC:**
1. Install [Miniforge](https://github.com/conda-forge/miniforge).
2. Open *Miniforge Prompt*, `cd` into this folder, run `build_windows.bat`.
3. Run `dist\AndySDR\AndySDR.exe`.

**Or run straight from source:** with Python 3.10+ installed: `pip install -r requirements.txt` then `python run_andysdr.py`.

## Updating AndySDR

AndySDR looks for a newer version on this project's GitHub Releases page a few seconds after it starts and then every
12 hours. When there is one, a pop-up shows what is new:

* **Download & install** – downloads the update (progress bar), checks its size and SHA-256 against what GitHub publishes,
  then asks **Install & restart**. AndySDR closes, updates itself and starts again (with `--server` if it was running that way).
* **Remind me later** – the pop-up returns next time AndySDR starts. **Skip this version** – not offered again until a newer one appears.
* **⚙ SETTINGS › Updates & system** has **Check for updates now** (also brings back a skipped version), a switch to turn the automatic
  check off, and *Download new versions in the background, then ask before installing*.

How it installs: a copy installed with `AndySDR-Setup.exe` runs the new installer quietly (Windows may show its usual permission
prompt if AndySDR is in *Program Files*); a portable (unzipped) copy has the new files copied over the old ones; a copy running from Python
source just opens the release page. Your settings, memories and pictures in `%APPDATA%\AndySDR` are never touched. The pop-up also appears
in a remote browser and the buttons work there too, but the update happens on the radio PC (the remote screen reconnects by itself).
Downloads are kept in `%APPDATA%\AndySDR\updates` and only ever come from `github.com/mrandyt/AndySDR/releases`.

**Publishing a new version (for you):** raise `VERSION` in `andysdr/config.py`, commit and push to `main`. GitHub builds the exe and
publishes release `v<VERSION>` with `AndySDR-Setup.exe` and `AndySDR-windows.zip` - existing copies then offer it. Pre-releases and drafts are ignored.

## One-time setup for your receivers

* **SDRplay (RSP2pro etc.)** – install *SDRplay API 3.x for Windows* from sdrplay.com. AndySDR finds `C:\Program Files\SDRplay\API\x64\sdrplay_api.dll` automatically. Close SDRuno/SDR# first – only one program can use the RSP at a time.
* **RTL-SDR / Nooelec** – run [Zadig](https://zadig.akeo.ie), *Options › List all devices*, pick *Bulk-In, Interface (Interface 0)*, install **WinUSB**.
* **Airspy / HackRF** – install the manufacturer's Windows driver.
* **Decoders** – nothing to do: DSD-FME, multimon-ng, SatDump and rtl_433 are bundled in the `decoders` folder next to `AndySDR.exe` (the installer also adds the Visual C++ runtime SatDump needs). The DECODERS tab shows a green tick for each one; if any shows a red cross, press **⬇ Download missing decoders** and AndySDR fetches it from GitHub.

## MeshCore messaging

AndySDR's receivers can only listen, so messages are sent and received by a small **MeshCore companion
board** (about £20–40: Heltec V3/V4, RAK WisBlock 4631, LilyGO T-Deck or T-Beam, Seeed Xiao…).

1. Flash it with **Companion** firmware from <https://flasher.meshcore.co.uk> – *USB Serial* if it will be
   plugged into the PC, *Bluetooth* or *Wi-Fi* otherwise (not Repeater or Room Server).
2. In AndySDR open the **MESHCORE** tab, choose *USB (COM port)*, pick the board's port (★ marks likely
   radio boards) and press **Connect**. For Wi-Fi enter `ip:port` (usually port 5000); for Bluetooth press
   **Find boards** (enter the PIN if one is set). AndySDR reconnects automatically next time.
3. If your board is new, press **Use UK preset** (869.618 MHz, 62.5 kHz, SF8, CR8 – what most UK nodes use),
   set a **Node name**, and press **Send advert** so others can see you.

Channels: *Public* is on every board; **+ Join** adds a public `#hashtag` channel (e.g. `#uk`) or a private
channel with a 32-hex-digit key. Contacts appear when nodes advertise – click one for an end-to-end encrypted
direct message (✓ sent, ✓✓ delivered). 869.4–869.65 MHz is licence-exempt in the UK; the board's firmware
keeps to the power and duty-cycle limits.

## Using it from another PC

1. On the radio PC, open **⚙ SETTINGS › Remote access** – it shows the address(es) and password.
2. Allow AndySDR through Windows Firewall when asked (*Private networks*).
3. On the other device, open the address in Chrome/Edge/Firefox/Safari and enter the password. Press **AUDIO ON/OFF** to hear it.

To run the radio PC without a window (e.g. a spare PC in the loft): `AndySDR.exe --server`.
Options: `--port 8045`, `--host 127.0.0.1` (this PC only), `--password NEWPASS`, `--browser`.
Keep AndySDR on your home network – do not port-forward it to the internet.

## RadioReference UK import

On radioreference.com open a county or agency page in the UK database and download its CSV
(premium feature), or select the frequency table on the page, copy it and paste it into a
text file. In AndySDR: **MEMORY › RadioReference UK import**, choose the file and a bank,
press **Import CSV**. Frequency, Alpha Tag, Description, Mode, Tag and Tone columns are recognised;
FMN/FM/AM/DMR/P25/NXDN/TETRA/POCSAG modes are mapped and the right decoder is attached.
`samples/radioreference_uk_example.csv` shows the format.

## ADS-B aircraft tracking

1. You need an antenna that works at 1090 MHz – a 6.9 cm whip (quarter wave) or a proper 1090 MHz collinear/ADS-B
   antenna, as high and clear as you can get it. VHF/airband antennas are poor at 1090 MHz. On an SDRplay RSP use the
   **Antenna A / B** (50 Ω) socket, not Hi-Z. A 1090 MHz filter/LNA close to the antenna helps a lot with long cables.
2. Open the **ADS-B** tab and press **▶ Start ADS-B (1090 MHz)**. AndySDR tunes to 1090 MHz, centres the receiver
   exactly on it (if the receiver was running below 2 MS/s it is switched to 2.4 MS/s) and starts the built-in decoder.
   Turn **RF GAIN** up fairly high; if you see plenty of messages but few aircraft, turn it down a little.
3. Aircraft appear within seconds. Type your **My position** (latitude/longitude in decimal degrees, e.g. `51.5074`, `-0.1278`)
   and press **Set** – you then get range rings, each aircraft's distance and bearing, and positions decode from a single
   message instead of needing a pair.
4. **Stop** (or tuning anywhere else) takes you back to normal scanning.

| Item | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Table | Callsign · hex (ICAO) address · airline (from the callsign) or country (from the address) · altitude · speed · heading · climb rate · squawk · distance from you · messages · time since last heard. Click a column title to sort, click a row to highlight it; faded = not heard for 30 s; rows disappear after 2 minutes. |
| Map | Plane icons point along their track and are coloured by height (orange low → green → blue high), with trails behind. Drag = pan, wheel / + − = zoom, **⌖ Fit** = show everything, click a plane for its details. The **Map** box picks a bright street map (default), a dark street map, or plain bright / dark grids that work offline. |
| Alerts | Squawk 7500 (hijack), 7600 (radio failure) and 7700 (emergency) flash red on the map and table and pop up a warning. |
| Last heard | **DECODERS › Last heard**: who was talking on DMR, P25, NXDN and M17 – time, system, timeslot, from, to, frequency and length, with the callsign and name from the RadioID list and talkgroup names from BrandMeister. Search box; **Export CSV** saves the whole list (opens in Excel; values that start with `=`, `+`, `-` or `@` are neutralised so a hostile callsign can't run as a formula). |
| Watch list | In the alerts card tick **Someone on your watch list is heard** and type callsigns, talkgroups or DMR IDs to watch (`M7FEP`, `TG235`, `2356184`), then press *Save watch list*. When one is heard a pop-up with a beep shows on the PC screen and the alert goes to the Remote app on your phone. The 🔔 on a Last heard row adds or removes that caller. The same caller on the same talkgroup is not announced again for 3 minutes. |
| Repeaters | **REPEATERS** tab: the RSGB list of UK repeaters (FM, DMR, D-STAR, Fusion), downloaded by the PC and refreshed every two weeks. | Sensors | **SENSORS** tab (needs the bundled rtl_433): choose **433.92 MHz** (UK / Europe) or **868.3 MHz** and AndySDR listens using its own receiver, then lists every sensor heard – weather stations, thermometers, tyre-pressure monitors, doorbells, remote sockets – with model, id, readings, battery and last heard. Search, **Export CSV**, *Clear*. Uses a fixed gain, so turn the RF gain down if the list stays empty. Experimental: tested with a stand-in program, not yet with real sensors. |
| Units | Aviation (ft · kt · NM) or metric (m · km/h · km) – remembered per computer. |
| Log / export | `%APPDATA%\AndySDR\adsb\adsb_YYYY-MM-DD.csv` gets one line per aircraft every 30 s; **Export CSV** saves the current list. |
| Sharing | In **⚙ SETTINGS › Sharing & logging** tick *Send the ADS-B feed over the network* to let other programs use AndySDR as their receiver: **SBS/BaseStation on TCP 30003**, **raw AVR on TCP 30002**. `http://<pc>:8045/api/aircraft.json?token=<password>` is a dump1090-style list. |

How it works: the radio thread passes the raw samples to a decoder thread that builds a 2 MS/s amplitude stream, finds Mode S
preambles (at two half-sample phases so no frame falls between samples), checks the 24-bit CRC (single-bit errors in
ADS-B frames from known aircraft are repaired) and decodes identification, airborne/surface position (CPR), altitude,
velocity, squawk and emergency state. Typical decode rate in the self-test is 97–98 % of simulated frames at any sample rate from 2 to 10 MS/s.
With the **Simulator** receiver you get ten practice aircraft around 51.5 N, 1.0 W (or around your own position).
Self-test: `python tests/test_adsb.py` (decoder) and `python tests/test_radio_adsb.py` (whole engine on the simulator).

## Aircraft text (ACARS, VDL2) and weather balloons

All three decoders are built in (no extra program to download).

* **ACARS** – the airlines' text messages with aircraft. Press the **ACARS** decoder key (or ▶ Start ACARS in the AIRCRAFT TEXT tab) on an AM channel around 131 MHz; in the UK 131.525, 131.725 and 131.825 MHz are the busiest. Each block shows registration, flight, label and message. Blocks are checked with the CRC and per-character parity; one or two damaged characters are repaired. A CSV per day is saved in `%APPDATA%\AndySDR\acars`.
* **VDL2** – the newer digital version of ACARS on 136.xxx MHz (D8PSK, 10.5 kbaud, 25 kHz channel). Press the **VDL2** key (or ▶ Start VDL2, or one of the 136 MHz buttons, in the AIRCRAFT TEXT tab); AndySDR switches to the 25 kHz digital mode and reads the signal itself. UK channels: 136.975 MHz (busiest), 136.725, 136.775, 136.825, 136.875 and 136.925 MHz. Frames are checked with the Reed-Solomon error correction and the frame checksum; the ACARS messages inside are listed in AIRCRAFT TEXT with a small VDL2 tag (the aircraft's address is in the tooltip and the CSV). Other VDL2 traffic (logon and routing frames) is counted but not shown. It needs a clean, fairly strong signal – a weak one gives fewer messages, not wrong ones.
* **Radiosondes** – press **SONDE** (or ▶ Start sonde decoder in the SONDES tab) on a balloon signal between 400.15 and 406 MHz; AndySDR switches to narrow FM, 16 kHz wide. Vaisala **RS41** sondes (the type the Met Office launches) are read: serial, GPS position, height, speed, climb, heading and satellites, with distance and bearing from you when *My position* is set. A CSV per day is saved in `%APPDATA%\AndySDR\sondes`. Other makes (DFM, M10, iMet) are not read yet.

ACARS and the RS41 were checked against real recordings (from the open-source acarsdec and rs1729 projects) as well as generated test signals. **VDL2 has only been tested against a transmitter written from the same protocol description** (no real VDL2 recording was available), so treat it as unproven until you have seen it read a real aircraft; none of the three has been tried on your own receiver yet.

## TETRAPOL

TETRAPOL is the French-designed digital radio system of the police and gendarmerie in France and of forces in Spain, Switzerland, the Czech Republic, Slovakia, Hungary and some other countries. **It is not used in the UK**, so you will only find it abroad (or by receiving remotely). Press the **TETRAPOL** key on a 12.5 kHz control channel (DIG mode), or leave Auto ID on: it recognises the 8000 baud GMSK signal and the 20 ms frame-sync pattern, corrects a mirrored spectrum by itself and starts the decoder. The log shows the cell (country code, network, location area), neighbour cells and call signalling. **Signalling only** – there is no free speech decoder, and most networks encrypt.

Status, stated plainly: the demodulator and the automatic identification are tested against generated signals only (no real TETRAPOL recording was available). The decoder program `tetrapol_dump` (Tetrapol-kit, GPL-2.0) is built by the Windows workflow with a build recipe and compatibility shims written without being able to compile it first, so if that build step fails the TETRAPOL key tells you the program is missing; everything else still works.

## Pager messages (POCSAG / FLEX)

1. Tune a pager frequency in **DIG** mode. In the UK most pager traffic is between about 153.0 and 153.5 MHz –
   the **Pagers** quick range in *LIVE ACTIVITY / SEARCH* finds the busy channels for you.
2. Open the **PAGER MESSAGES** tab and press **▶ Start pager decoder** (or leave *Auto-identify* on and AndySDR
   starts it when it recognises a pager signal). multimon-ng decodes POCSAG 512, 1200, 2400 and FLEX at the same time.
3. Each page appears as a new row at the top:

| Column | What it shows |
|---|---|
| ★ | Click to highlight that capcode: its rows are coloured and a notice pops up whenever it is paged. *★ Highlighted only* hides the rest. |
| Time / Frequency | When it was received and the frequency (click the frequency to tune there). |
| Protocol | POCSAG 512 / 1200 / 2400 or FLEX 1600 / 3200 / 6400. |
| Capcode | The pager's address (POCSAG adds the function number, e.g. `123456/3`). Click it to show only that capcode. |
| Name | Your name for the capcode – click **+ name** to set or change it. Names are saved and used in exports. |
| Type | *Alpha* (text), *Numeric* or *Tone* (a beep with no message). |
| Message | The decoded text. FLEX group pages list the other capcodes they went to. |

* **Search** looks in the message, capcode, name and frequency. *Protocol*, *Type* and *Hide tone-only* narrow the list further.
* **Export CSV** downloads the list (with names). **Clear list** empties the screen; log files are kept.
* Every message is also saved to `%APPDATA%\AndySDR\pager\pager_YYYY-MM-DD.csv` (one file per day), and today's
  messages come back when AndySDR restarts.
* The same page heard again within a minute (repeat transmissions, or two POCSAG speeds catching it) is listed once.
* The raw multimon-ng output still appears in the DECODERS tab log.

Remember that in the UK it is an offence to act on, or pass on, the content of pager messages you are not
authorised to receive – see *Legal* below.

## Weather pictures

* **HF WEFAX** (built in): UK Met Office Northwood on 2618.5, 4610, 8040 and 11086.5 kHz. Tune the
  frequency in USB, start the WEFAX decoder – charts appear in **WEATHER IMAGES**. Needs HF reception
  (the RSP2pro covers it; RTL-SDR needs direct-sampling mode or an upconverter).
* **Meteor-M LRPT**: Meteor-M N2-3 and N2-4 transmit on 137.9 MHz (sometimes 137.1 MHz), 72k.
  (Meteor-M N2-2's LRPT is no longer working.) Use DIG25 mode, start the LRPT decoder when the
  satellite rises and stop it when it sets; SatDump then makes the pictures. A V-dipole or QFH antenna helps a lot.

* **Automatic passes**: use **SCHEDULER › Satellite auto-capture** instead of watching the clock – see *Satellites* above. Enter your position once (it is shared with the ADS-B position if you set that first).
* **GOES HRIT** (1694.1 MHz, GOES-19 East at 75.2°W and GOES-18 West at 137°W): needs a 1 m-class dish, an L-band LNA and a clear view of the satellite. From the UK GOES-East sits right on the horizon, so AndySDR reports it as below your horizon – there is nothing to receive. Decoded with SatDump through a local rtl_tcp link to your receiver. (LRIT on 1691 MHz was replaced by HRIT on the GOES-R satellites.)
* **SSTV** (built in): ISS events on 145.800 MHz NFM, 2 m 144.500 MHz NFM, 20 m 14.230 MHz USB, 40 m 7.165 LSB.
  Leave Auto ID on, or start the SSTV decoder – pictures appear in **Weather images**. Use the **Scheduler** to catch them while you're out.

## Where things are stored

## Legal (UK)

The Wireless Telegraphy Act 2006 allows listening to broadcast, amateur and CB transmissions; it is an
offence to listen to other transmissions you are not authorised to receive or to disclose their content.
AndySDR cannot decode encrypted signals (e.g. Airwave TETRA: you will see the network and signalling, not the speech). Check the law where you live.
